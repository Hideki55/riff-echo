    How the yardstick works

    One bar of 4/4 laid out like a ruler. Tall marks are the beats (1 2 3 4), medium marks the “&”s, short marks the “e” and “a” 16ths, so you can see how far apart the notes are.

    Each groove is eight two-note building blocks: HO thumb then hammer-on, TT two thumbs (some charts write EE), ST left-hand slap then thumb, PT pop then thumb, TP thumb then pop.

    Rhythmic modes: loop the groove until every sound is even. Then use Block mode to start on the 2nd, 3rd … 8th block, and 16th shift to slide the whole pattern one dash so a different sound lands on beat 1. Tap any block on the yardstick to swap it.

    Extra block (off by default, not part of Wellington’s five): PO, pop then hammer-on, since a hammer-on can start from a popped note as well as a thumbed one.

    “Muted” means you still slap or pop while the left hand rests on the strings, so you get a percussive dead note (outlined circles). “As taught” plays thumbs on open E and hammer-ons D→E, with ST, PT and TP muted. Concept from Anthony Wellington (Bassology) and Victor Wooten’s Groove Workshop.

    Saved slap riffs

      How the Creator works

      Slap, Bass, Guitar: pick an instrument at the top. Each has its own players, sound, settings and saved riffs. Bass and guitar riffs use the same phrase logic as slap (call and response, a pop-style hook, pickups and anticipations) but are played fingerstyle or with a pick, so the rhythm yardstick starts folded away and the tab is the main view. Bass players: James Jamerson, Justin Chancellor (Tool), Jaco Pastorius, Rocco Prestia and Carol Kaye. Guitar: Nile Rodgers, Tony Iommi, Jimmy Page, Jimi Hendrix and Angus Young. They’re style sketches, not transcriptions.

      Slap riffs use the same building blocks as the rhythm drills, with real notes from the key and mode. Thumbs (T) take the low notes on the E and A strings, favouring the root and fifth. Pops (P) take the high notes on the D and G strings, favouring the octave, third, fifth and seventh. Hammer-ons (O, joined by an arc) start from a thumbed note on the E or A string, a popped note on the G string, or either on the D string. They go up one or two frets, or from an open string up into the hand position. Everything sits in one four-fret hand position around the root, shown on the fretboard.

      In the style of picks rhythm cells, note choices, tempo and drum beat typical of each player’s approach (for example octave pops for Larry Graham, linear 16ths for Mark King (every 16th filled with left-hand slaps and dead thumbs, ringing notes on syncopated spots or in groups of three), open-string pedals and odd intervals for Les Claypool). They’re style sketches, not transcriptions of anyone’s songs.

      Length: a 1-bar riff answers itself (beats 3–4 echo beats 1–2 with a new ending). With 8 bars, bars 5–6 bring a new call (different rhythm, starting on the 4th) before the opening call returns and the line ends on the root. With 2 bars, bar 1 is the call, ending away from the root so it sounds unfinished, and bar 2 is the response: it echoes the call’s rhythm on different notes (leaning on the 4th or 5th) and comes home to the root. 4 bars play call, response, call, then a busier final response. Dead notes add percussion between the pitched notes.
